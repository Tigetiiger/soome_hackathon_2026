SMART Weight & Insight: a weight for every compactor, an answer for every customer

The plan in seven sentences

  1. Europress's compactors already know how full they are, but nobody on the customer's side knows how heavy the waste is, even though every invoice and every contract is priced per tonne.
  2. We propose to turn each SMART-connected machine into an independent weight estimate, computed from the fill level, ram pressure and weather data the machine already produces, and anchored by a small group of machines fitted with real load cells and by the operators' weighbridge tickets.
  3. On top of that weight, the SMART Insights dashboard we built in the hackathon gets seven additions: the weight engine itself, a five-answer customer home page, nightly sales briefs, a pickup feed with tonnes for operators, a fleet map with weather, a quality panel and a reporting pack.
  4. Fixed rules make every decision, Claude only writes the explanations with citations, and a person approves anything that leaves the system, so the weight stays a neutral second opinion and never becomes a bill.
  5. The Answers tier is free as a hook for the roughly 85 % of customers not yet on SMART, Verified Weight costs €15 per machine per month for chains and fixed-schedule customers, and operators get the feed free in exchange for sharing their tickets.
  6. Expected add-on revenue is about €0.9M a year by 2029, but the real case for Europress is stickier SMART subscriptions and service contracts, which turn a negative stand-alone NPV into a positive one.
  7. The roadmap starts with software only in November 2026, runs a €0 test of archived pressure data against tickets before any hardware is bought, and stops at any phase gate that fails, so the most Europress can lose is the Phase 0 budget of about €270k.

Start here · Who is involved, what exists, what we change, how it gets built

The whole proposal on one screen, for a reader who has seen nothing else

This page assumes no background. It starts with who the players are and what already exists, then lists the seven concrete changes, each with what the user will see afterwards and how it is built. Everything further down is the detail behind this section.

Who the players are, and what exists today

Europress

A Finnish maker of waste compactors and balers: machines that press waste into a container on the customer's site. It sells and leases the machines (a lease runs about 6 years) and sells service contracts, which it calls its real profit. About 12,000 machines are in the field assumption. Tommi is the Europress representative who briefed the hackathon teams twice.

SMART

Europress's connected service. A SIM in each machine sends fill level, compaction starts, motor time and the hydraulic pressure curve. SMART predicts when a machine will be full and orders the pickup; SMART Center staff watch machines remotely. Three levels exist: Intro (basics), Insights (analytics) and Integrated (connected to the customer's or operator's systems). About 15 % of customers use it. SMART knows how full a machine is. It does not know how heavy the waste is.

The waste operator

A separate company (about 20 in Finland) that sends the truck, empties the container, weighs the load on a weighbridge at its plant and bills the customer per tonne. Europress is not a party to that contract. Operators are also Europress customers, because they lease machines too. The practice data has five invented ones, Hauler Alpha to Epsilon.

The customer

Shops, hotels, hospitals, factories and property managers. They pay the operator's invoice, usually on a 3-year contract followed by a tender. They cannot see the weight they pay for unless the operator shares its weighbridge ticket. The demo customer on this page is "Hospitality Customer 0049", a hotel group with 3 sites and 9 machines.

The dashboard we built

"SMART Insights", a working web app built during the hackathon in Finnish and English on the practice data. Nine tabs today: Overview, Savings, Machines, Operators & contracts, Weight & seasons, Trends, Account, For waste operators, FAQ, plus a floating SMART Advisor (Claude, read-only). A Python pipeline writes one JSON file per customer in which every number carries its formula and source rows. A small Node server serves it and runs the advisor. The front end is plain JavaScript with Chart.js.

The practice data

Europress's synthetic competition pack, "SMART Material Value Engine v1.0.1": 52 invented customers, 5 operators, hourly telemetry per machine from October 2025 to September 2026, collection events, weighbridge tickets, quality assessments, maintenance work orders, contracts, price lists and invoice lines. Nothing in it is a real Europress figure. Table names on this page, such as operations/collection_events, refer to it.

The weight model today

The dashboard already has a first "virtual scale", versions v1 to v3. It multiplies fill level by container volume and a density per material and season, then corrects each machine with its own weighbridge tickets. On 3,522 test pickups the median error is 7.0 %, and 63 % of pickups land within ±10 %. 345 machines are calibrated; machines at the operator that shares no tickets are not. It uses no pressure and no weather yet.

Weight studies behind this plan

Two separate team studies feed section 4. A compactor simulator generated 400 virtual machines with physics for moisture, stiffness and ram pressure, and a model trained on it reaches 4.7 % mean error on machines it has not seen. A hardware study priced load cells and modelled the cases where pressure cannot see the mass; it reaches only about 29 % at P95 from pressure alone. The plan treats both as unproven until the first real test.

TermMeaning here
Pickup, lift, emptyingOne visit where the operator takes the container away to empty it. The practice data calls it a collection event.
Weighbridge ticketThe slip from the operator's truck scale: gross weight, tare, net kg. Some operators share it with the customer, some do not.
TareThe weight of the empty container, subtracted from the gross to get the waste weight. A stored tare that is out of date makes every ticket wrong by the same amount.
Fill at emptyingHow full the machine was when the truck came. SMART aims for 85 % or more. A truck that empties a 49 % full machine is a half-empty pickup the customer still pays for.
Fixed schedule vs on-callFixed: the truck comes on set days whether the machine is full or not. On-call: SMART orders the truck when the machine reaches a threshold, typically 75–85 %.
Soft sensor, virtual scaleA weight estimate computed from data the machine already has (fill, ram pressure, strokes, temperature, weather). No scale is fitted.
Ram pressureThe hydraulic pressure the compactor's ram needs to press the load. Denser or wetter waste needs more pressure at the same fill.
Load cellA sensor that measures force. Four under a compactor's supports make a scale platform. Classed C3 under OIML R60; IP68 means sealed against water.
Reference cohortA small set of machines with real load cells. Their true weights train and check the soft sensor on every other machine.
StreamThe kind of waste in a machine: cardboard, paper, plastic film, mixed plastic, mixed waste, biowaste.
Rejected loadA load the plant refuses to recycle because it is contaminated. It is burned, and the customer pays a fee.
SMART CenterEuropress's remote-monitoring and support team.
Traced numberA number in the dashboard that opens to show its formula, assumptions and the data rows it came from. Every number in the pipeline is stored this way.
NAWI, MID, OIMLThe EU directives and international recommendations that govern scales used for billing. A "second opinion" weight is not covered by them; a billing scale is.
FMI, ERA5The Finnish Meteorological Institute, whose open data is free for commercial use. ERA5 is a global weather reanalysis, used on this page for the mockups only.

0 · How we decided

Six rules behind every choice on this page

Most of this plan follows from a few rules we set after the first briefing and tightened after the second. Every section below has a Why box that says what we chose, what we rejected and where the reason comes from. Section 12 collects all of them in one log.

1 · Build on what Europress already has

The machines, the SMART platform, Vincit's code, the scale platform and the service network already exist. We add to them rather than start a new business, because Europress makes its profit on SMART and service, not on data.

Kick-off briefing · plan 04 decision 2

2 · Weight, not fill

Fill prediction already works, and other teams built more of it. Europress said "everything is about weight" and that it only estimates it. That is the gap nobody in the market fills.

Kick-off briefing · section 5

3 · Rules decide, Claude explains, a person approves

On our benchmark, fixed rules beat a machine-learning classifier (macro-F1 0.73 against 0.35). Rules can be audited and explained to an operator. Claude only writes text, with citations, and nothing leaves the system without a person.

Team output 04 · risk table

4 · A second opinion, never a bill

Legal-for-trade weighing needs certified hardware and verification. A second opinion needs none of that, and it keeps Europress neutral between a customer and an operator who is also a Europress customer.

NAWI and MID directives · section 4

5 · Simplify: answers first, details on click

Tommi said 60–70 % of the information in reports is ignored, and asked for "instructions for dummies". So every screen starts with one sentence and a colour, and the formula sits one click deeper.

Second briefing

6 · Tag every number, and let Europress replace it

The competition data is synthetic and the prices are invented. Rather than hide that, each number carries its source tag and each assumption is visible, so a pilot can swap in real figures without rewriting the plan.

2 · The add-on

Seven modules on one data spine

Every module reads the same data: SMART telemetry, the weight estimate, weather, operator tickets and contracts. Fixed rules make every decision. Claude only explains and drafts, and a person approves anything that leaves the system. Each module has a buyer and a price, or a clear reason to be free.

Why seven modules on one spine, and why three of them are free

One spine because every module needs the same five inputs. Building them as separate products would mean five copies of the same pipeline, and the demo dashboard already has the traced-JSON pipeline the spine extends.

Seven modules because each maps to one voiced problem in section 1 and has its own buyer. Fewer would bundle buyers with different budgets; more would be features, not modules.

Free where the buyer would not pay. Answers (M2) is free because the median practice-data customer saves €0, and it is the hook for the ~85 % of machines not on SMART. Sales briefs (M3) are internal because one extra percentage point of SMART adoption pays more than any licence. The operator feed (M4) is free for operators who share tickets, because Europress told us it does not sell data, and the tickets are what calibrates the weight.

Each card below carries a one-line Why of its own.

Rejected: customer invoice upload with AI reading (Tommi: point-to-point links with thousands of customers are "probably not feasible"; the ~20 operators are the integration point). Our own contamination detection (licensed vision models exist). Automatic actions without approval. A headline about recovering overbilling. The name "TrueLoad", which is already a truck-overload product.

One pickup through the whole system

To show how the modules connect, here is one pickup followed end to end: machine CMP-00434, a cardboard compactor at Lieto site 3 of the demo hotel group, collected by Hauler Delta on 2 October 2026. The pickup, fill level, estimate, range and tare age are the ones in the operator-feed mock in section 3e. The hourly telemetry values and the ticket weight are illustrative, because the practice data holds no ticket for this operator.

  1. 1 Oct, 14:00 · telemetryThe machine reports as it does today. Fill 79 %, 212 ram strokes this cycle, peak pressure 168 bar, oil 31 °C. SMART's existing fill prediction orders a pickup, because this machine is on on-call at 75 %. Nothing new yet.
  2. Same hour · M1 weight engineThe pipeline turns that reading into a weight. It builds the features (last fill, 6-hour median fill, last and 24-hour peak pressure, strokes this cycle, and 23 mm of rain in Lieto over the last 7 days from FMI) and the model returns 1,400 kg, range 1,240–1,560 kg. The machine card in the dashboard shows "≈ 1.4 t inside".
  3. Same evening · M4 operator feedDelta's planner sees the pickup in their own system with expected_kg: 1400 and tare_age_days: 412. The second field is a hint that the stored tare is over a year old and worth re-weighing.
  4. 2 Oct, 09:40 · the liftThe tilt sensor and GPS register the lift, as they do today, and a collection event is written. The dashboard records fill at emptying as 79 %: a good pickup, not a half-empty one, so no early-lift cost is added to the customer's cost card.
  5. 2 Oct, 11:15 · the ticketDelta weighs the load at its plant: net 1,510 kg. Today Delta shares no tickets, so this number never reaches the customer. In the plan, Delta shares tickets in exchange for the feed, and the ticket arrives through the operator API the same day.
  6. Night of 2 Oct · reconciliation ruleThe rule compares ticket and estimate. 1,510 kg is inside the range, so the pickup is marked "checked, agrees". Had the ticket fallen outside the range on 3 of the last 10 pickups, Delta would get a note first ("please re-weigh container CMP-00434's tare"), and after five working days the customer's contract card would show "needs a second look".
  7. Same night · M2 answersThe five cards re-run. The cost card is unchanged. The contract card still shows Delta at 0 % ticket sharing, so it stays red, and because it was already red no new email goes out.
  8. Same night · M3 sales briefThe rule "operator shares no tickets and notice date within 90 days" is true for this customer, so Claude's nightly draft uses this pickup as the example: "even yesterday's cardboard pickup could not be checked against a ticket". The salesperson approves it on Monday and it becomes a CRM task.
  9. Days later · M6 qualityThe plant grades the load (minor contamination). The grade joins this ticket and appears on the Lieto 3 quality panel; the advisor can say what to sort better.
  10. Quarter end · M7 reportingThe 1,510 kg counts as recycled cardboard in the Q4 export, with its treatment code and transport CO₂, traced back to this event.

3 · Integration into the existing dashboard

Same three workspaces, fewer clicks to an answer

The SMART Insights dashboard already has three workspaces: Customer, Europress and Partners. We keep them, follow Tommi's "simplify, simplify, simplify", and put answers before reports. New pieces are marked NEW; changed ones UPGRADE. The mockups below use the demo customer's practice-data records and real 2025–26 weather.

Why we kept the three workspaces and changed what is inside them

Customers, Europress staff and operators already know the three workspaces, and each one has its own login scope. Replacing them would cost learning time for no gain, and "simplify, simplify, simplify" argues against a fourth.

What changed is the order inside each one. Today the Customer workspace opens on savings charts; the new home opens on five answers because Tommi asked for ready-made answers rather than a page the user has to interrogate. Reports stay, one level down, for the people who want them.

The mockups use the demo hotel group and real ERA5 weather so that each screen can be checked against numbers in the practice data rather than invented ones. Each mockup below has its own Why box, closed by default.

Rejected: a separate "Weight" product with its own portal. A new login for a feature nobody asked for is the opposite of simplifying.

3a · Customer home: five answers, then drill down

This is Tommi's framework, word for word: cost, safety, CO₂, "everybody working according to the contract", and recycling. Each card is one sentence a non-expert understands. Select a card to see what the customer sees next. The same rules send an email when a card turns red.

Why five cards, in this order, with one sentence each

The five topics are Tommi's own list from the second briefing: cost, safety, CO₂, contract, recycling. We did not add or merge any. Cost comes first because the user brief said the money must explain itself at first sight, and because it is the card that leads to a sale.

One sentence and one colour, because 60–70 % of report content is ignored. Red, amber and green instead of scores, because a non-expert can act on a colour and cannot act on a percentile. A red card sends an email because Tommi described exactly that: "a flag to the office that says you should go to the customer".

The contract card is worded as a neutral scorecard, never as an accusation, because the operator sees the same card first (section 8) and the practice data showed most device-versus-ticket gaps had causes on the weighbridge side, not fraud.

Rejected: a single savings number as the home page (it hides the levers), and a chat-first home (it forces the user to know what to ask).

3b · Fleet map with weather

Europress and operators see the whole fleet; a multi-site customer sees only its own sites. Each dot is a municipality with sites in the practice data, at its real location. Choose what colours the dots. "Fill at emptying" shows where trucks drive half-empty, which is where the sales case is. "Weather" shows real monthly ERA5 conditions at each region's centre. Production uses MapLibre with National Land Survey or Digitransit vector tiles. This mock is plain SVG because the page cannot load map tiles.

Why a map, why municipalities, and why "fill at emptying" is the default layer

A list of 34 municipalities says less than their positions do. Lapland's remote surcharges and the Pohjois-Pohjanmaa frost both live on a map. Dots sit at municipality level because that is the finest location the practice data gives, and because a chain's procurement team thinks in regions, not device IDs.

"Fill at emptying" is the default because it is the sales case: red dots are where trucks drive half-empty and where SMART on-call pays for itself. Tonnes and weather are secondary layers because they explain rather than sell.

Rejected: a live tile map in this document (the page cannot load tiles), and site-level dots (fictional sites have no real coordinates).

3c · Weather that changes what people do

Weather is a feature in the weight model and a trigger for alerts. The chart shows real daily weather in Helsinki over the practice-data year: mean temperature as a line, rain and snow as bars, freezing days shaded. Below it are four alerts the rules would send (the storm alert is illustrative). In production the source is FMI open data, which is CC BY 4.0 and allows commercial use. Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial, so this page uses it only for the mock.

Why weather is both a model feature and an alert trigger, and why FMI

Both briefings raised the same unknowns: cardboard in winter versus summer, frozen biowaste, rain in open hoppers. The simulator showed wet open-hopper cardboard weighs 4–6 % more, so weather belongs in the weight model. Over a whole year it changes the average error very little (+0.01 pp without it), but it matters on the wet and frozen days when a customer's bill is wrong. The same data predicts the events people can act on, which is why the alerts share one pipeline.

FMI is the production source because its open data is CC BY 4.0 and allows commercial use at no cost. Open-Meteo, which this mock uses, is non-commercial on its free tier. MET Norway is the fallback because it covers Finland under the same licence class.

Alerts are deliberately rare and concrete. Each one names a site, a cause and an action, because a generic "bad weather coming" would be one more ignored notification.

Rejected: a paid weather API (no benefit over FMI for Finland), and forecasting waste volumes from weather alone (weather is an explanation, not the main signal).

3d · Weight lab in 3D, for Europress engineers and SMART Center

3D earns its place here because the question has three variables: fill level and ram pressure against weighed mass. Fill alone cannot tell a light load from a heavy one. Pressure separates them, so at the same fill, the heavier loads sit at higher pressure. Drag to rotate. The points are 1,200 simulated pickups from our compactor simulator. In production the same view plots each reference machine's real pickups, so engineers can watch for drift and recalibrate. Customers never see 3D. They get one number with a range.

Why 3D here, and nowhere else

Everywhere else on the page a 2D chart would do. Here the question is three-dimensional: at the same fill level, a wet or dense load sits at a higher ram pressure than a dry one, and only the third axis shows that. Engineers need the shape of that cloud to see when a machine drifts and needs recalibration.

It lives in the Europress workspace only, because customers asked for simplicity. They get one number with a range. The library loads only on this tab, so the rest of the dashboard stays light.

Rejected: 3D views for customers (impressive, not useful), and a 3D digital twin of the compactor now (planned later with three.js, once real load-cell data exists to drive it).

3e · Operator feed: the next 14 days, with tonnes

Bramidan's BRA-IN already sends collection schedules to haulers. Our feed adds the expected weight of every pickup, so the operator can plan truck capacity and its weighbridge. It is free for operators who share their weighing tickets back.

Why the feed lands in the operator's ERP, and why it is free for tickets

Operators plan in AMCS, WasteHero or Ecomond, not in a Europress portal. Tommi's line was "we don't need to reinvent the wheel, but there are ways to connect those together", so the feed is JSON, CSV or a webhook, with field mappings for the systems they already use.

Tonnes are the difference from BRA-IN's schedule feed. A hauler that knows the expected weight can fill trucks and plan the weighbridge, which no fill sensor gives.

It is free in exchange for weighing tickets because tickets are the anchors that calibrate the soft sensor, and because Europress said it does not sell data. A trade keeps both sides' lawyers calm.

Rejected: an operator portal of our own, and charging for the basic feed (we would have paid for the tickets anyway).

3f · Sales brief: "tell me what to tell the customer"

      
Tommi asked for this directly: salespeople want clear answers, not reports. Every night, rules rank the customers. Claude then writes three sentences per customer, each citing the data behind it. A salesperson edits and approves the brief before anything goes out.

    
Why rules rank, Claude writes, and a salesperson approves
Tommi asked for "tell me what I should tell the customer", not for a dashboard salespeople must read. Three sentences is what fits in the opening of a phone call.
Rules do the ranking because they can be explained to a salesperson in a line ("notice date within 60 days"), and because on our benchmark rules beat the learned classifier. Claude writes the sentences because that is what a language model is good at, and every sentence cites the rows behind it so the salesperson can check. Approval is mandatory because the risk table's worst case is Claude telling a customer something wrong.
Cost is not a factor: a brief costs cents, and the advisor is already capped at $0.50 per answer.
Rejected: fully automatic outreach (trust, and operator relations), and Claude doing the ranking (not auditable).

3g · Waste quality: start with the operator's grades, add cameras later

    
Tommi called quality "our next exercise", and said weight plus quality would put Europress "in a super position". Cameras are not needed to start, because operators already grade loads at the plant: the practice data holds 6,721 such grades. Stage 1 shows these grades per machine, and Claude explains what to sort better. Stage 2 adds a hopper camera only to mixed-waste and plastic machines, where rejection fees are real.

    
Why quality starts with grades that already exist, and cameras wait
Tommi called quality "our next exercise" and said weight plus quality would put Europress "in a super position". The cheapest first step is the data operators already produce: the practice pack holds 6,721 plant assessments. Showing them per machine costs a week of work and no hardware.
Cameras come second, and only on mixed-waste and plastic machines, because those are the streams with rejection fees. On cardboard a camera would cost more than the fees it prevents. Compology's price of about $30 per camera per month sets the ceiling for what customers accept.
In the live dashboard we show accepted versus rejected rather than A–D grades, because Tommi told us plants do not grade finely: "either it's total crap or it's fine". This plan keeps the grades only as the operator's input.
Rejected: building our own vision model (licensed models from WasteVision or Greyparrot exist), and a camera on every machine.

3h · How it plugs into the current code

    
Today's dashboard has four parts: pipeline/build_data.py builds traced JSON, a Node server serves it, the front end is vanilla JS with Chart.js, and a read-only headless Claude acts as the advisor. The add-on keeps that shape. It adds five pipeline steps, three server routes, and two front-end libraries that load only on the tabs that use them.

    
Why we keep the current architecture instead of rebuilding
The demo dashboard was built in the hackathon with a traced-JSON pipeline, a thin Node server, vanilla JavaScript and a read-only Claude sandbox. That shape already passed a security review, and Vincit can take it over without a framework migration. Adding five pipeline steps and three routes is weeks of work; a rewrite is months.
The two new front-end libraries load only on the tabs that need them, so customers who never open the map or the lab pay nothing in load time.
Rejected: a React or Vue rewrite (no user-facing gain), and a separate microservice for weight (one more thing to deploy for a model that runs in seconds).

4 · Weight and hardware
A ladder of accuracy: free for everyone, load cells only where they pay

  
Tommi suggested a hybrid: "a very cheap, maybe even one sensor somewhere, which would bring some kind of a reference point, and then this trained model". The ladder does exactly that. A small reference cohort carries real load cells. Every other machine runs the trained soft sensor, calibrated against that cohort and against operator tickets.

  
Why a ladder instead of one answer, and why we publish our own disagreement
Load cells on every machine would cost €3.4–6.3k each, more than most customers can save. A soft sensor alone has no anchor and drifts. Tommi proposed the middle path himself: "a very cheap sensor somewhere as a reference point, and then this trained model". The ladder is that proposal written down as tiers, with each machine on the cheapest tier that pays.
The reference cohort is about five machines per operator and region because drift depends on the material, the operator's weighbridge and the climate. One national cohort would miss a Lapland frost or a single operator's stale tare.
We show the conflict between our two studies because hiding it would be the costliest mistake in this plan. If the hardware study is right, the soft sensor is a rough estimate and the money moves to T1 and T2. The €0 archive test in Sprint 0 answers this before anyone buys hardware.
Retrofitted machines get a rough estimate, labelled as rough, rather than nothing, because customers have mixed fleets and an honest rough number beats a gap in the report.
Rejected: legal-for-trade weighing as the default (certification cost, and it makes Europress the billing party), and claiming the weight "gets more accurate with every pickup" (it does not, without tickets).

How the T0 soft sensor is built, step by step
  1. The machine already sends, every hour over its SIM: fill %, number of ram strokes, peak hydraulic pressure, oil temperature and the lift flag.
  2. One firmware change: log the peak pressure of every stroke, not just the hourly peak. Pressure is what tells a heavy load from a light one at the same fill.
  3. The pipeline builds features for each machine before each pickup: last fill and the 6-hour median; last pressure, 24-hour maximum and 48-hour median; strokes and hours in this cycle; mean oil and air temperature; and rain and humidity for each of the last 7 days from FMI.
  4. A gradient-boosted model (LightGBM, about 800 trees) predicts net kg. It is trained on pickups with a true weight: load-cell pickups from the reference cohort and shared weighbridge tickets. It is tested on machines it has never seen, which is the number in the stat at the top of this page.
  5. The range comes from conformal calibration: the model's errors on held-out pickups set the width so that about 80 % of true weights fall inside it. Snowfall and rain widen it.
  6. Each machine gets an offset from its own last tickets (this is tier T1). A drift alarm fires when 3 of the last 10 ticketed pickups fall outside the range.
  7. Monthly retrain, with the version shown in the dashboard as it is today (v1 to v4). The accuracy figure is published on the FAQ page.

    
How a T2 reference machine is fitted
  1. Four IP68 C3 shear-beam load cells go under the compactor's support points, with a junction box and a summing indicator in the existing control cabinet. A tilt sensor rejects readings on uneven ground.
  2. Readings are taken at rest only: ram idle, no lift in progress, no person on the platform.
  3. Automatic tare: when GPS and the tilt sensor say the empty container has returned, the first idle reading becomes the new tare. This is the error the operator's stored tare has, and the reason tare age is in the feed.
  4. The value goes to SMART over the same SIM as the fill level. No new connectivity.
  5. Cost €3.4–6.3k installed per machine, one day of fitting. The first two are fitted and checked at the Kerava test rig with 3 tonnes of test weights before any customer site.
  6. Where they go: about five machines per operator and region, chosen to cover each stream, so that drift from material, weighbridge and climate is all seen. That is 2–5 % of the fleet.

  

  

    
Our studies disagree, and we say so. On our simulator, the trained soft sensor (fill, pressure, strokes and weather) reaches 4.7 % mean error on unseen machines, though only 61 % of pickups land within ±5 %. The separate hardware study models physics where water and stiffness change the mass without changing the pressure. There, pressure-only estimates reach only about 29 % at P95. The truth lies between the two, and only real data can settle it. That is why Phase 0 starts with a €0 test: run archived SMART pressure curves against shared tickets.

    
Cheap sensors that help the model assumed costs
Oil-temperature probe, €20–40, because cold oil reads high. Hopper-lid switch, about €10, to tell rain ingress apart from material. Hopper temperature and humidity sensor, about €15, for frozen biowaste. Firmware logging of every ram stroke, €0. Retrofitted machines from other makers report only a "full" lamp. They get a rough fill × stream-density estimate, labelled as rough.

  

  
Legal position. Our weight is a second opinion for checking invoices, never the basis for billing. Static platform scales fall under the NAWI directive (2014/31/EU, OIML R76). Weighing in motion on a truck or compactor usually falls under OIML R51 and the MID (2014/32/EU). Tier 3 needs verification by Kiwa (notified body 0424), repeated every 3 years. We will ask Tukes to review the "second opinion" wording before launch.






  
5 · Market and existing solutions
Everyone measures fullness. Nobody gives the customer the weight.

  
We compared connected-compactor and waste-data products. All of them measure how full a container is, and some route trucks. We found no commercial product that estimates compactor weight without a platform scale, only patents. Every existing weight belongs to the operator. A weight that belongs to the customer is Europress's opening.

  
Why these products, and what the comparison decided
We looked at every connected-compactor and waste-data product we could find with a public price or feature list, in the Nordics, the US and the operator software market. The question was not "who is best" but "does anyone already give the customer a weight". Nobody does without a platform scale, so the plan competes on weight and leaves fill, routing and bale counting to the products that already do them.
Three rows set prices: Nordsense shows customers accept a per-sensor subscription, Compology sets the ceiling for a camera tier, and Tamtron's certified truck scales are the ground truth we calibrate against rather than compete with.
Rejected: positioning against Bramidan and Orwak on fill features (a feature race SMART does not need), and treating operator portals as competitors (they are the integration partners in section 7).

6 · Business model and economics
Free hook, paid weight, gain-share where price wins

  
Europress told us it sells tools and service, not data: "the customer owns their own data". Every line below is therefore a subscription to a tool or service. Prices are our assumptions and should be replaced with real SMART prices. No fee is more than a third of what the customer can save.

  
Why €15, why a free tier, and why we tell the board the subscription alone does not pay
€15 list, €12 in a lease because the fee must stay under a third of what the customer saves, and because the parallel session reached the same number independently. The draft plan argued for €8; at €8 the contribution is about €3.20 per machine per month and break-even needs about 3,500 paying machines, more than half the realistic market. Real SMART prices should replace both.
A free Answers tier because the median practice-data customer has nothing to save. Charging them would lose the hook for the ~85 % of machines not yet on SMART. The paying buyers are chains and fixed-schedule customers, where the savings are real.
Gain-share in price-driven markets because in Poland and Benelux the cheapest offer wins. A fee paid only out of savings already banked cannot lose a tender.
The honest NPV. The subscription on its own has a six-year NPV of −€0.33M. We say so because a board sold a "new revenue line" would cancel the project at the first gate. The real case is retention: +5 pp on service renewals and higher SMART attach turn it to +€0.23M. Weight is a reason to stay with Europress, not a product line.
The corrected cohort cost. Plan 04 put the reference scales at €10–19k. The bill of materials, written later, prices four IP68 C3 load cells per machine at €3.4–6.3k, so ten machines are €34–63k. We show the correction rather than the old number.
Rejected: €25 per machine (above a third of value for most customers), charging operators for the basic feed, and selling anonymised data (Europress does not sell data, and competition law limits it).

Build little, connect what already works

Each partner covers something Europress should not build alone. Vincit already owns the SMART code, so a second vendor would split the stack. 8 · Communication plan
Who hears what, how and how often

  
Operators are partners, and they are Europress customers too. The rule: findings about an operator go to that operator first, with five working days to explain, before the customer sees them as "needs a second look". We never write "overcharged" or "fraud".

  
Why operators hear first, and why two words are banned
The ~20 Finnish operators are Europress customers too, and they hold the tickets the weight needs. A product that looks like a weapon against them would lose both. So every finding about an operator goes to that operator first, with five working days to explain, and only then to the customer as "needs a second look". The practice data supports this: most device-versus-ticket gaps had ordinary causes, such as an old stored tare.
"Overcharged" and "fraud" never appear because they are legal claims Europress cannot prove from an estimate, and because one such word in a customer email would end an operator relationship.
The customer email is four sentences because Tommi asked how we would explain it "to your grandmother". The board gets a gate deck, not a monthly report, because the only board decision is go or stop.
Rejected: publishing an operator league table (competition-law risk, and operators would stop sharing tickets), and daily customer notifications (ignored within a month).

9 · Roadmap with stop signs
Ship software first, prove weight next, then scale

  
Each phase has a test. If the test fails, we stop rather than keep spending.

  
Why software first, Finland first, and a stop sign at every phase
Software first because Sprint 0 runs on data SMART already collects and costs nothing in hardware. It proves two things before any money is spent: that salespeople use the briefs, and that archived pressure curves predict ticket weights. If either fails, the plan changes while it is still cheap.
Finland first because the operators, FMI, Kiwa, Tukes and the pilot chains are all here, and the team can sit in Kerava at the test rig. Poland and Benelux come second because they are price-driven markets where Proof of Fullness fits, and Poland's BDO register gives an independent cross-check.
Gates because the two weight studies disagree. A fixed roadmap would commit €270k on a number that may be wrong. With gates, the most Europress can lose is the Phase 0 budget, and each gate's test is a number already defined in section 1.
Certified T3 weighing comes last because it only pays for customers who want to bill from their own scale, and because verification needs a product that already exists.
Rejected: starting with hardware (the intuitive order for a machine company, and the expensive one), and launching all markets together.
